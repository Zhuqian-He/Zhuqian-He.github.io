> *"How do institutional frictions and social norms shape the allocation of human capital and the returns to it?"*

I am interested in how institutions and social norms shape individuals’ education, career, and family decisions over the life cycle, particularly for women. My current work focuses on gender disparities in scientific credit, recognition, and knowledge production, while I am also exploring related questions in labor markets, organizations, and family economics.

More broadly, I am interested in understanding to what extent observed differences in individual choices and outcomes reflect underlying preferences and productivity, and to what extent they reflect institutional and social constraints on how talent is allocated, evaluated, and rewarded.

---

### Research Questions

#### 1. 🔍 Labor Supply & Career Outcomes
How do workplace institutions, caregiving responsibilities, and family constraints shape labor-force participation and career trajectories?

#### 2. ⚖️ Allocation & Recognition
How are talent, tasks, credit, and rewards allocated within organizations and labor markets, and when do distortions in these processes affect incentives and productivity?

#### 3. 📜 Norms & Life-Cycle Decisions
How do social norms and gender expectations interact with economic incentives to shape education, career, location, marriage, and fertility decisions?

#### 1. Gender and Scientific Credit Allocation
*Status: Work in Progress (Jan. 2026 – Present)*

* **Overview:**  
  This project investigates whether female scientists receive systematically less scientific credit than their male co-authors within the same paper.

* **Preliminary Empirical Findings:**
  * **Gender Gap Across Team Sizes (Figures 1 & 2):** Across author team sizes ranging from 2 to 20, female authors have lower average credit scores than male authors in unconditional between-paper comparisons (Figure 1). A similar gender gap remains when comparisons are restricted to co-authors within the same paper (Figure 2).
  * **Persistence Over Time (Figures 3 & 4):** Across 5-year cohorts from 1950 to 2024, the gender gap in measured credit is visible in both between-paper comparisons (Figure 3) and within-paper co-author comparisons (Figure 4), suggesting that the pattern is not confined to recent cohorts.
  * **Robustness Across Specifications (Figure 5):** The estimated gender difference remains negative and statistically significant across specifications ranging from bivariate OLS to models incorporating paper-level controls, author career trajectories, year and field fixed effects, and paper fixed effects.

#### 2. Whose Economics? Gender Composition, Perspective Differentiation, and Complementarity in Economic Knowledge Production
*Status: Research Proposal*

While economic phenomena motivate scientific inquiry, they do not uniquely dictate how research questions are framed or which mechanisms are prioritized. This project investigates whether economists with different gendered social and institutional experiences systematically bring distinct analytical perspectives to comparable gender-related economic phenomena, and whether such differences generate intellectual complementarities in co-authorship.

I propose a conceptual framework of knowledge production in which heterogeneous lived experiences govern the cognitive salience of underlying economic mechanisms. To test the empirical implications of the model, the proposed design will analyze gender-related articles published in leading economics journals (including the Top Five and premier applied field journals) from 2000 to the present. Specifically, it will compare the framing of closely related questions in solo-authored papers by male versus female authors against co-authored work, with a particular focus on mixed-gender collaborations. Using text-as-data methods, I also plan to construct authors’ pre-collaboration perspective profiles to examine whether mixed-gender teams combine more distant analytical perspectives and whether such ex ante diversity is associated with greater conceptual recombination.

More broadly, this project asks whether diversity in scientific inquiry operates not only through differentiated technical skills, but fundamentally through heterogeneity in the analytical frameworks researchers bring to economic science.

#### 3. Bridging Recognition? Women-Focused Scientific Awards and the Allocation of Scientific Prestige
*Status: Research Proposal*

Women-focused scientific awards are intended to recognize female scientists who may be underrepresented in mainstream prestige systems, but their downstream effects remain unclear. This project asks whether receiving a major women-focused scientific award increases the probability of, or accelerates the timing of, subsequent recognition by higher-prestige, gender-neutral awards.

To formalize this intuition, I propose a two-period model of Bayesian updating and attention allocation under costly evaluation. In an environment where underlying scientific quality is imperfectly observed, targeted awards can alter evaluators' posterior beliefs through a certification channel and reallocate scarce evaluative attention toward previously overlooked work.

Empirically, I plan to test the model's implications using risk-set matching to compare recipients with observationally similar female scientists who have not yet received the award, balancing on dynamic pre-award scientific and career trajectories. Furthermore, I plan to analyze official award citations to determine whether subsequent mainstream recognition honors contributions completed prior to the targeted award or later output, allowing me to distinguish between a pure recognition mechanism (certification and visibility) and a productivity-enhancing pathway (resource accumulation).
